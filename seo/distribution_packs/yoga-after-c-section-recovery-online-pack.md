<div class="blog-section">
<h2 class="section-title">Phase 1: Weeks 0-6 (Rest and Breath)</h2>
<p>In the first six weeks following a c-section, the primary focus is pure rest and the initial stages of recovery. During this time, the body is recovering from major abdominal surgery, and nothing should load the abdomen.</p>
<p>Your practice in Phase 1 consists entirely of:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Diaphragmatic Breathing:</strong> Gentle breathing that encourages the ribs to expand and helps reconnect with the deep core without strain.</li>
<li><strong>Ankle Pumps:</strong> Simple foot and ankle movements to promote circulation and prevent stiffness while resting in bed.</li>
<li><strong>Gentle Pelvic-Floor Activation:</strong> Soft, subtle contractions and releases to begin waking up the pelvic floor muscles, always within a pain-free range.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="blog-section">
<h2 class="section-title">Phase 2: Weeks 6-12 (Gentle Rebuilding)</h2>
<p>Once you have reached the 6-week mark and received official medical clearance from your doctor, you can slowly begin to reintroduce movement. The goal in this phase is rebuilding deep core strength without creating intra-abdominal pressure that could compromise your recovery.</p>
<p>Safe practices for Phase 2 include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Supine and Side-Lying Poses:</strong> Movements where the back is fully supported, minimizing gravity's pull on the abdominal wall.</li>
<li><strong>Supported Bridges:</strong> Using blocks to gently lift the hips, engaging the glutes and pelvic floor without overarching the lower back.</li>
<li><strong>Gentle Cat-Cow:</strong> Very small, controlled spinal movements to restore mobility, ensuring there is no aggressive stretching of the front of the abdomen.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="blog-section">
<h2 class="section-title">Phase 3: 12+ Weeks (Progressive Loading)</h2>
<p>As you progress beyond 12 weeks, the focus shifts to progressive loading, gradually reintroducing functional strength movements. This phase should only be started when the incision line is entirely pain-free and your core can manage the internal pressure.</p>
<p>Practices to slowly integrate in Phase 3:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Modified Planks:</strong> Practising planks from the knees or elevated against a wall to reduce the load on the linea alba.</li>
<li><strong>Low Lunges:</strong> Gentle stretching and strengthening of the hip flexors and legs.</li>
<li><strong>Standing Sequences:</strong> Slowly building back to standing poses like Warrior II and gentle balancing postures.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="blog-section">
<h2 class="section-title">Red Flags: When to Stop and Call Your Doctor</h2>
<p>Throughout your recovery, it is crucial to listen to your body and recognize signs that you may be pushing too hard or that an issue needs medical attention. If you experience any of the following red flags during or after practice, <strong>stop and call your doctor immediately:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Doming or Coning:</strong> If you see a bulge or ridge pushing up along the center of your abdomen (the linea alba) during any movement, stop immediately. This indicates your core cannot manage the pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Incision Pain:</strong> Any sharp, pulling, or aching pain at or near your c-section scar.</li>
<li><strong>Bleeding:</strong> Any return or increase in vaginal bleeding (lochia) or bleeding from the incision site.</li>
<li><strong>Dizziness or Lightheadedness:</strong> Feeling faint or dizzy during movement.</li>
</ul>
<p>Always err on the side of caution. Working alongside your doctor's care is paramount for a safe and healthy recovery.</p>

<h2 class="section-title">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="faq-item">
<h3 class="faq-question">When can I start yoga after a c-section?</h3>
<div class="faq-answer">
<p>Most practitioners are advised to wait until around 6 weeks and medical clearance before anything beyond breathing practice; the first 6 weeks are for diaphragmatic breathing and rest only. Your surgeon's or obstetrician's guidance always overrides any general timeline.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="faq-item">
<h3 class="faq-question">Which yoga poses should I avoid after a c-section?</h3>
<div class="faq-answer">
<p>Until cleared and progressively rebuilt: deep twists, strong core work such as boat pose and full planks, deep backbends, and anything that causes doming along the incision line. Doming is your stop signal.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="faq-item">
<h3 class="faq-question">Is yoga safe with diastasis recti after a c-section?</h3>
<div class="faq-answer">
<p>Gentle, diastasis-aware practice can support recovery, but conventional crunch-style core work can worsen separation. Work with a practitioner trained in postnatal-safe movement alongside your physiotherapist or doctor, and stop any pose that produces doming or pain.</p>
